<h2>Statutory Coverage – Required by Law </h2>				
		<p><b>In plain language:</b> Statutory coverage refers to the minimum amount of insurance legally required for certain types of liabilities. Just like a driver needs to have minimum coverage to legally drive cars, many businesses also have minimum coverage requirements. <br /></p><p><b>Technical definition:</b> In the context of insurance, the term "statutory" refers to the minimum requirements imposed by law on insurance policies. This typically applies to liability coverage such as workers' compensation or auto liability, and the exact requirements can vary by state and specific policy conditions. </p><p>Imagine you're involved in a car accident. Your insurance claim could be impacted if you don't carry the statutory amount of auto liability coverage required by your state. </p>

					<h2>What Is Statutory Coverage in Insurance? </h2>				
		<p>Statutory coverage refers to the minimum amount and types of insurance that law requires individuals or entities to maintain. While these requirements may vary depending on the location and type of exposure, the primary goal remains the same: to provide a baseline level of protection to the insured party and any potential third-party claimants. <br /></p><p>For instance, in the USA today, every state has statutory minimum amounts for auto liability insurance to ensure that drivers can cover potential damages they might cause. Similarly, many businesses must provide statutory workers' compensation insurance for their employees. These requirements are dictated by statutes—laws enacted by legislative bodies—and all applicable parties must follow them. </p><p>However, remember: meeting statutory requirements should be the starting point, not the end goal. True risk management often involves securing coverage beyond what's legally required. </p>

					<h2>Common Questions About Statutory Coverage </h2>				
		<p aria-level="3"><b>What happens if I don't have statutory coverage?</b> </p><p>If you lack the statutory coverage, you could face legal penalties including fines and suspension of licenses or permits. You also open yourself up to increased liability, as you won't have adequate insurance to cover potential claims. Failure to meet statutory standards can present a significant risk. </p><p aria-level="3"><b>How does statutory coverage apply to businesses?</b> </p><p>For businesses, statutory coverage often refers to required workers' compensation insurance. As per law, businesses must have this coverage for their employees. If a restaurant, for instance, lacks this coverage and a chef gets injured, the restaurant could face substantial financial penalties. </p><p aria-level="3"><b>Can statutory requirements change?</b> </p><p>Yes, statutory requirements can change, primarily because they're tied to laws, which can be updated or revised. For instance, a recent congressional action could raise minimum coverage requirements for certain insurance types. </p><p aria-level="3"><b>How do I know what my statutory requirements are?</b> </p><p>The statutory requirements will depend on your location, your liability exposure, and your line of business. Your insurance agent or broker can help you understand these requirements, but it's also a good idea to consult with a qualified attorney or legal entity familiar with your industry's regulations. </p>
