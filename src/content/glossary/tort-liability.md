<h2>Tort Liability – Legal Responsibility for Damages </h2>				
		<p><b>In plain language:</b> Tort liability is the legal term for when someone has to pay damages for causing harm or damage to another person due to their actions. This could mean they were careless (like causing a car accident), intentional (like slander), or strict (like owning a wild animal). <br /></p><p><b>Technical definition:</b> Within the context of tort law, tort liability refers to the legal obligation of a defendant (the person or entity being sued) to pay monetary compensation to the plaintiff (the person suing) for harm or damage they have caused. This damage can result from intentional torts, negligence torts, or strict liability torts, all of which are delineated within the common law tradition. The specific forms of tort and their relevance would depend on the tort law system in effect and the nature of the civil wrong committed. </p><p>Imagine your morning cup of coffee causing a third-degree burn because the cup was too hot. This is a classic example of how the tort system in tort law comes in play to serve justice. </p>

					<h2>What Is Tort Liability in Insurance? </h2>				
		<p>The web of tort law can be complex, but the kernel of the concept is consistent: tort liability deals with civil wrongs and how compensation is determined and handed out. The defendant, whose civil wrong has resulted in harm or loss to the plaintiff, is usually held responsible for monetary compensation, as determined by the specific rules around tort cases. This often varies by state and carrier; always check the specific policy form. <br /></p><p>While a tort is often taken to imply negligence, it extends well beyond this. Hence, tort liability can also take other forms, such as intentional tort, where the harm was deliberately caused, or strict liability, where the defendant is held liable regardless of culpability if certain dangerous situations result in harm. </p><p>Tort liability is paramount in insurance as insurance carriers often step in to handle claims and coverage in lawsuits involving torts. Thus, the tort law system is a key fixture in any liability and negligence disputes. </p>

					<h2>Common Questions About Tort Liability </h2>				
		<p aria-level="3"><b>What is the difference between intentional tort and negligence?</b> </p><p>While both intentional tort and negligence fall under tort law, the difference lies in intent. Intentional torts are civil wrongs where the defendant's actions were deliberate, such as assault, battery, or trespassing. Negligence, on the other hand, involves a breach of duty of care that leads to harm but is absent of any intention to cause harm. An example of negligence tort could be a pedestrian accident caused by a driver's carelessness. </p><p aria-level="3"><b>What is meant by strict liability?</b> </p><p>Strict liability is a legal doctrine that makes an individual or company liable for their actions regardless of their intent or negligence. For instance, in a strict liability tort case, a manufacturer could be held accountable with product liability tort for manufacturing defects even if they had taken all necessary precautions. </p><p aria-level="3"><b>What is the primary purpose of tort law?</b> </p><p>The primary purpose of tort law system is to provide relief to injured parties for harms caused by others, to impose liability on parties responsible for the harm, and to deter others from committing harmful acts. It provides a venue – civil court, where the plaintiff can sue the defendant for damages. </p><p aria-level="3"><b>Can liability insurance protect against tort liability?</b> </p><p>Yes, liability insurance is designed to protect policyholders from tort liability. It can cover litigation costs from tort cases, legal liability for damages and settlements, and any related legal fees, subject to policy terms, limits, and exclusions. </p><p aria-level="3"><b>What are some examples of tort claims?</b> </p><p>Examples of tort claims can include situations where the plaintiff seeks compensation for personal injury, property damage, emotional distress, privacy invasion, defamation, false imprisonment, and medical malpractice, among others. </p>

					<h2>Real Claim Examples Involving Tort Liability </h2>				
		<p><b>Scenario 1: </b>After a retail store failed to display a caution sign for a wet floor, a customer slipped and fell, sustaining injuries. In this tort of negligence, the store held the duty of care and thereby, the tort liability, resulting in them having to pay the customer’s medical bills, loss of earnings, and other related costs. <br /></p><p><b>Scenario 2: </b>A popular toy manufacturing company faced a product liability tort, an example of strict liability tort, when it was discovered a toy they manufactured contained harmful, choking-hazard parts for children. Despite rigorous quality checks, the company was held responsible for all harm caused by its product. </p><p><b>Scenario 3: </b>A case of intentional tort involved a corporation that intently spread misinformation about a competitor, causing significant lost revenues. A business tort resulted, and the offending company was held liable for the lost revenue and additional punitive damages. </p>

					<h2>How to Explain Tort Liability to Clients </h2>				
		<p><b>Personal Lines client </b>"Think about tort liability as needing to pay for an accident you've caused. This could be your car colliding into someone else's car or if your dog bites a neighbor. But it could also be situations where you didn't directly cause an accident but are still to blame, like a guest tripping on your broken front porch steps." <br /></p><p><b>Small Business owner </b>"Let's say a customer slips on ice outside your property.
