					<h2>What Is Classification Codes in Insurance? </h2>				
		<p><b>Plain-language definition</b>: In insurance, a Classification Code is a number that represents the type of work a business does. This helps insurance companies decide how much to charge for insurance. </p><p><b>Technical definition</b>: Classification Codes are specific codes derived from systems like NAICS (North American Industry Classification System) or SIC (Standard Industrial Classification) used in the insurance industry to categorize the business operations of policyholders. They typically appear within policy declarations and are used to calculate premium based on the level of risk associated with each class of business. </p>

					<h2>Common Questions About Classification Codes </h2>				
		<p aria-level="3"><b>What's the difference between the NAICS and the SIC system?</b> </p><p>NAICS replaced the SIC system in the U.S. during the late 1990s because it provides a more detailed classification structure. The SIC system is still used in some cases, particularly by insurers and agencies that have not made the change yet. </p><p aria-level="3"><b>How often should a business's class code be reviewed?</b> </p><p>Class codes should be reviewed at every renewal and any time there's a significant business change. Don’t wait for the premium audit – that’s too late. For example, if you know a restaurant started offering delivery due to COVID-19 restrictions, that's a substantial change in exposure and might warrant a different class code. </p><p aria-level="3"><b>Are premiums largely impacted by these codes?</b> </p><p>Definitely. Classification codes directly impact the workers' compensation rates because they reflect the likelihood of workplace injury. A wrong class code can result in incorrect premiums – either higher or lower than what they should be. </p><p aria-level="3"><b>Wait, my client’s business does more than one thing, how is this handled?</b> </p><p>A business can have multiple class codes. If you look at a contractor, they might have one code for carpentry work, one for masonry work, and another code for clerical office workers. </p>

					<h2>Real Claim Examples Involving Classification Codes </h2>				
		<p><b>Scenario 1: </b>A small construction company primarily did insulation work, and their class code reflected this. They took a big project which involved roofing work but didn't inform their agent. During the project, a worker fell off the roof and sustained severe injuries. The claim was initially denied because roofing was not covered under their class code. <br /></p><p><b>Scenario 2: </b>A restaurant with a primary classification code for operations without liquor was incorrectly classified as such because they added a sports bar. Failing to update their class code led to a significant liquor liability claim being denied as the insurer had rated and issued a policy on incorrect information.  </p><p><b>Scenario 3: </b>A manufacturing business had grown from a single operation to multiple disparate operations, including a separate delivery service. The company was still paying premiums appropriate for a manufacturing operation – a relatively lower risk. A vehicle accident resulted in a substantial claim, and the insurer questioned why they weren't informed about the delivery operation. The audit led to a significant premium increase. </p>
