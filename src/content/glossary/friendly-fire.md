<h2>Friendly Fire – The unintentional harm caused to ones' own side in a conflict. </h2>				
		<p><b>In plain language: </b>Think of friendly fire like the accidental shots in a paintball game, but in a real-life military context. It means when someone accidentally hurts their own team members during a fight or conflict. <br /></p><p><b>Technical definition: </b>In military terminology, friendly fire refers to unintended harm or damage caused by one's own forces during combat operations. This generally happens due to mistaken identities, miscommunication, or lack of coordination within ground forces. It can occur in all types of military operations, from infantry training to high-intensity engagements. Moreover, this term is not exclusive to gunfire and can include any accidental fire resulting in casualties, like a 40mm grenade detonating in the wrong place, or artillery fire misaimed. </p><p>Friendly fire, despite its misnomer, brings to mind the devastating reality of war casualties caused not by enemy forces but from within ones' own ranks. From famous incidents in World War II and Vietnam War to the Gulf War; the urgency to prevent such incidents is crucial in every fight. </p>

					<h2>What Is Friendly Fire in Insurance? </h2>				
		<p>In the insurance context, friendly fire refers to the accidents or damages incurred not by an external event or force, but due to an individual’s own actions or negligence. Much like the fog of war on a battlefield that heightens the risk of miscommunication and miscoordination, the complexity of insurance policies and a lack of clear understanding or care can inadvertently lead to self-inflicted losses. <br /></p><p>The term typically appears in exceptions or conditional clauses within policy wording. Strict adherence to safety procedures or improved communication might eliminate most of these avoidable losses. </p>

					<h2>Common Questions About Friendly Fire </h2>				
		<p aria-level="3"><b>What can cause friendly fire?</b> </p><p>Friendly fire is often caused by miscommunication, mistaken identities, lack of response inhibition, or accidents during combat operations. For example, an M-79 grenade launcher might mistakenly identify an allied tank as an enemy due to infrared lights not working properly. </p><p aria-level="3"><b>What are the notorious examples of friendly fire?</b> </p><p>World War II saw multiple friendly fire incidents, like when U.S. anti-air unit shot down an American F-15. In the Gulf War, a U.S. Patriot Battery mistakenly identified a British plane as an enemy. Friendly fire in Kuwait resulted in a British tank attacking another British tank due to miscommunication. </p><p aria-level="3"><b>How is friendly fire prevented in combat?</b> </p><p>Preventing friendly fire involves multiple strategic and tactical measures. Full recognition training for armored vehicles and helicopter crews’ personnel, careful planning and coordination, strict fire discipline, and effective communication systems are necessary. For instance, phosphorescent thermal tape can be used to mark friendly troops or vehicles. </p><p aria-level="3"><b>What is the implications of friendly fire incidents?</b> </p><p>Friendly fire incidents can have severe implications, both physically and mentally. They can lead to unwarranted war casualties, diminish the morale of the troops and cause significant combat stress. </p>

					<h2>Real Claim Examples Involving Friendly Fire </h2>				
		<p><b>Scenario 1:</b> During a search and destroy mission in World War II, due to the fog of war, an infantry unit was accidentally fired upon by their own machine gun emplacement. The friendly fire was only realized when recognition flares were started by panicked soldiers. Speed up recognition training and signaling equipment improvements were initiated post the event. </p><p><b>Scenario 2:</b> In a machine gun training exercise, a machine gun was misfired, causing collateral damage to a nearby trench filled with troops. The ensuing investigation revealed that the incident occurred due to a faulty firing mechanism, revealing prevention measures should include equipment checks and precautions as well.  </p><p><b>Scenario 3:</b>  During Vietnam War, a claymore mine, set up as part of an ambush patrol, detonated closer to guard rotation than intended, causing multiple injuries amongst friendly troops. This devastating friendly fire incident provided a stark reminder of the strict adherence to trip flares and detonation location calculations. </p>
