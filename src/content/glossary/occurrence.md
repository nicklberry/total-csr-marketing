<h2>Occurrence – An event that triggers insurance coverage </h2>				
		<p><b>In plain language:</b> An occurrence in insurance refers to an event or incident that results in a covered loss for an insured party. Think of it like a car accident that triggers your auto insurance coverage. <br /></p><p><b>Technical definition:</b> An occurrence, in the context of insurance, is an incident or event that inflicts damage, harm, or loss and thus triggers the policy's coverage. This term typically appears in the insuring agreement of policy forms and is central to establishing whether a particular claim falls within the policy period. Commonly seen in General Liability and Professional Liability policies, it's essential to clarify the context and wording of "occurrence" per policy document and the covered perils. </p><p>Is a lost piece of jewelry a covered incident, or does a dog bite qualify as an event triggering the coverage? Not all incidents are occurrences; understanding the difference is vital in dealing with claims. </p>

					<h2>What Is Occurrence in Insurance? </h2>				
		<p>Occurrence is a critical term in insurance policies, usually stipulated in the policy declarations or its insuring agreement. It explains the incident or event that results in a loss or damage covered by the policy. Some typical examples include car accidents, property damages, or injuries - essentially, any harmful event that can trigger the policy's coverage. <br /></p><p>ISO standard form policies often define an "occurrence" as an accident, including continuous or repeated exposure to the same harming conditions. However, this often varies by state and carrier; always check the specific policy form. </p><p>Occurrence connects to broader coverage concepts like "occurrence-based policies" and "claims-made policies," which define policy coverage boundaries based on when the occurrence took place and when the claim was reported, respectively. </p><p>An essential distinction to be aware of is that not all incidents nor all claims are necessarily classified as occurrences. </p>

					<h2>Common Questions About Occurrence </h2>				
		<p aria-level="3"><b>What's the difference between occurrence and claims-made policy?</b> </p><p>In an occurrence policy, coverage applies to losses from incidents that occur during the policy period, regardless of when the claim is reported. In contrast, under a claims-made policy, the insurer covers claims reported during the policy period, regardless of when the loss-causing event took place. </p><p aria-level="3"><b>Can a loss be covered if it's not a 'defined' occurrence?</b> </p><p>That depends on the specifics of your policy. Some policies list specific "defined perils" or occurrences they cover. If the event causing the loss is not one of these defined perils, then the loss typically won't be covered. </p><p aria-level="3"><b>Are all incidents considered occurrences?</b> </p><p>Not necessarily. The incident must result in some form of insured loss to be considered an occurrence. Also, the definition and interpretation of "occurrence" might vary among different insurance companies. </p><p aria-level="3"><b>Is a continuous leak considered one occurrence or multiple occurrences?</b> </p><p>A continuous or repeated leak can be considered as one occurrence if it originated from the same source or cause. </p>

					<h2>Real Claim Examples Involving Occurrence </h2>				
		<p><b>Scenario 1: </b>A professional services firm held an occurrence-based policy covering losses from injuries at their premises. During a firm event, an employee slipped, injuring her wrist. The claim was covered as the event (accident) occurred within the policy period. <br /></p><p><b>Scenario 2: </b>A homeowner experienced a roof leak during a heavy snowfall. His homeowner's insurance defined this as an "occurrence" (weather-related event causing damage), and the policy covered the repair cost. </p><p><b>Scenario 3: </b>In a commercial building, tenants complained about continuous mold issues due to a leaky pipe. Because the leak originated from the same issue over time, the insurer regarded it as a single occurrence, hence simplifying the claim process.</p>
